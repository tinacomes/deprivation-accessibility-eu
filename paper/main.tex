\begin{abstract}
\noindent
Proximity has become the planning paradigm of European cities: the
15-minute city promises every daily need within a short walk and is
advanced as a route to urban resilience. That promise concerns everyday
life. Urban populations also face a growing burden of emergencies, with
heat alone killing an estimated 44,000 Europeans a year and the number
exposed to extreme heat projected to rise from ten million to almost 300
million a year under three degrees of warming; in an emergency what
matters is whether care reaches people in time. Accessibility research
measures both kinds of need as travel time, which cannot say how much a
resident loses when a service lies further away. Deprivation theory,
developed in humanitarian logistics to quantify the welfare lost during
time without an essential good, can. We bring that theory into urban
analysis and ask whether the deprivation cities impose follows the same law
for everyday and for emergency needs. Measuring both on a 100\,m population
grid for 67 functional urban areas in 24 European countries, we find two
regimes that obey different laws. Everyday deprivation falls with city
size while emergency deprivation does not, and everyday inequality widens
with size while emergency inequality never narrows. Emergency deprivation
follows the coverage of national emergency systems rather than city size:
five cities that appear average by travel time carry 2.4 to 4.7 times the
typical emergency deprivation. Within cities, the neighbourhoods most
deprived of everyday services are also, more often than chance, the most
deprived of emergency care, and neighbourhoods with many children carry
this compounded deprivation most consistently. A city that works every day
is not thereby resilient.
\end{abstract}

\bigskip
\noindent\textbf{Significance statement.}
Cities are planned for everyday proximity, yet urban populations
increasingly face emergencies in which minutes decide survival. We show
that everyday and emergency needs form two regimes of urban deprivation
that obey different laws. Measuring anchored deprivation rather than
travel time across 67 European city regions, larger cities reduce everyday
deprivation but not emergency deprivation, everyday inequality grows with
city size, and emergency deprivation follows national coverage, with five
capitals harbouring emergency deserts that travel-time averages cannot
detect. The same neighbourhoods, often those with many children, lose in
both regimes. A city that works every day is not thereby resilient;
urban resilience requires both regimes.

\bigskip
\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} urban deprivation; accessibility; 15-minute
city; emergency medical services; urban resilience; spatial equity; urban
scaling

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}
% ==========================================================================

Three quarters of Europeans live in urban areas, a share projected to
exceed 83\,\% by 2050 \citep{eea2024urbanadaptation}, and the cities they
live in are increasingly planned around one idea: that every daily need
should lie within a short walk. The 15-minute city has become the planning
paradigm of the continent \citep{moreno2021fifteenminute,allam2022fifteenminute},
and it describes an everyday regime, in which residents choose when to
reach a general practitioner, a pharmacy, a supermarket, a school or a
park. Urban life is also lived in a second regime. Heat kills an estimated
44,000 Europeans a year today, a toll projected to triple by 2100 under
current policies \citep{garcialeon2024mortality}; about ten million
Europeans are exposed to extreme heat each year, a number that reaches
almost 300 million, more than half the population, under three degrees of
warming \citep{naumann2020heat}; and the burden falls on cities, where
2.3 million climate-related temperature deaths are projected across 854
European cities by 2099 without adaptation \citep{masselot2025cities}. The
summer of 2026 opened with the earliest intense heatwave on record
\citep{copernicus2026mayheat}. The emergency regime is exercised every
day and not only in disasters: across Europe about 8\,\% of people who
suffer a cardiac arrest outside hospital survive, the ambulance reaches
them after 12 minutes on average, and each minute of delay lowers the
probability of survival \citep{grasner2020eureca,grasner2025eureca3,larsen1993survival}.
The European Union has responded by asking member states to plan for
emergencies explicitly, down to 72 hours of household self-sufficiency
\citep{ec2025preparedness}. Everyday life and emergencies place different
demands on the same city. What does a resident lose when the services they
need lie further away, and does the answer differ between the everyday
and the emergency regime?

Urban research has one established answer, and it is a measurement rather
than a theory. Accessibility, defined by Hansen as ``a measurement of the
spatial distribution of activities about a point''
\citep{hansen1959accessibility}, is operationalised as travel time. The
15-minute-city programme \citep{moreno2021fifteenminute,papadopoulos2023compliance},
global travel-time mapping of health care and daily services
\citep{weiss2020healthcare,wu2025global} and the measurement of unequal
access within cities \citep{nicoletti2023disadvantaged} all count minutes,
and a minute counts the same wherever it falls, for whichever service, for
whichever resident. Combined with urban scaling, whose empirical
regularity \citep{bettencourt2007growth} and generative theory
\citep{bettencourt2013origins} hold that infrastructure and services
improve systematically with city size, the access position implies a
prediction: larger cities should serve every need better, everyday and
emergency alike, because the returns to scale that concentrate facilities
do not distinguish between service classes. The position cannot say how
much a resident loses per minute, and it has no reason to expect two
service classes to behave differently.

Welfare economics offers a second position. Deprivation theory, developed
in humanitarian logistics, defines the deprivation cost of a good as the
welfare lost during the time a person spends without it, and shows that
this cost is estimable as a function of deprivation time
\citep{holguinveras2013objective,holguinveras2016econometric}. Two objects
follow, and their difference carries this paper. For substitutable goods
the loss is a \emph{level}: bounded and S-shaped, saturating once people
adapt and substitute \citep{wang2017deprivation}. For critical goods the
loss is a \emph{cost}: convex and unbounded, rising ever more steeply with
time, with estimates for goods and specifically for emergency ambulance
services \citep{cantillo2018discrete,macea2018attitudes,delgadolindeman2019ambulance}.
The value-of-time literature of transport economics, built on routine
trips whose delay can be rescheduled, is the wrong valuation for the
second object: the clinical evidence that survival after cardiac arrest
falls by one to two percentage points per minute of delay
\citep{larsen1993survival,nicholl2007distance} is a statement about
mortality. Deprivation theory therefore predicts what the access position
cannot: because the welfare lost follows the need, the everyday and the
emergency regime need not obey the same law, and a city may serve one
while failing the other. Urban resilience research reaches the same
distinction from the other side. Resilience has been defined through
equitable access to essential services
\citep{meerow2016defining,logan2020reframing}, the 15-minute city is
advanced as a resilience strategy \citep{moreno2021fifteenminute}, and
yet everyday vulnerability profiles have been shown to fail as predictors
of crisis-time capability \citep{sirenko2026heatwaves}. Social
vulnerability research adds the question of who carries the burden
\citep{cutter2003vulnerability}. Whether the neighbourhoods and
populations that a city fails every day are also those it fails in an
emergency is, in that literature, an open empirical question.

The stakes of choosing between the two positions are not academic. By the
standard access statistic, the median drive time to emergency care,
Bucharest is served better than the typical European city (8.5 minutes,
0.71 times the sample median). By deprivation cost it carries 2.8 times the
typical burden, because a well-served core pulls the median down while a
deep periphery lies far beyond any clinical response benchmark. Which
number a planner sees decides whether Bucharest has an emergency problem
at all. In this paper we measure which of the two positions describes
urban Europe.

We do so for 67 functional urban areas (FUAs) in 24 European countries,
from 101{,}050 to 12.9 million inhabitants, drawn by a stratified design
over four macro-regions and four size strata, on a common 100\,m population
grid. Europe is the setting for three reasons. First, comparability:
Europe has a harmonised functional city definition \citep{dijkstra2019fua},
a harmonised 1\,km census grid, near-complete street and facility mapping,
and clinical emergency-response benchmarks shared across national systems
\citep{pons2005response,blanchard2012response,peleg2004ems}; global
travel-time mapping exists \citep{weiss2020healthcare,wu2025global}, but
it cannot carry a welfare calibration or a census-anchored vulnerability
analysis. Second, maturity: large cities lose their growth advantage as
urban systems mature \citep{musso2026cities}, and Europe is the mature end
of that life cycle, so its cross-sectional size gradient reads what city
size delivers once growth no longer favours the large city. Third,
obligation: access to services of general economic interest and to
essential services is a right in the European legal order
\citep{eu2012charter,eu2017pillar}, emergency care is organised nationally
under shared response targets, and the Preparedness Union Strategy now asks
member states to plan for the emergency regime explicitly
\citep{ec2025preparedness}. The sample therefore covers one polity with one
set of obligations and 24 national emergency systems. Following the scaling
tradition we estimate log-log elasticities of city outcomes on population
\citep{bettencourt2007growth} under the harmonised city definition
\citep{musso2026cities}; every size gradient is cross-sectional, and any
phrase such as ``as cities grow'' means the space-for-time reading of that
gradient, never a longitudinal observation.

Three findings follow. First, deprivation is a different object from
access: it explains the cross-city structure better than travel time,
agrees with travel time wherever results are rank-based, and reveals what
travel-time averages hide, five emergency deserts in European capitals.
Second, urban growth delivers on one clock only: everyday deprivation falls
with city size and emergency deprivation does not, everyday inequality
widens with size and emergency inequality never narrows, and emergency
deprivation follows the coverage of national emergency systems rather than
city size. Third, the two deprivations compound: in 66 of 67 cities the
neighbourhoods most deprived of everyday services are also, more often
than chance, the most deprived of emergency care, most strongly in
Central-Eastern Europe, and neighbourhoods with many children carry the
compounded burden most consistently.

\section{Results}\label{sec:results}
% ==========================================================================

Two surfaces summarise each city; their construction is in Materials and
Methods, and Fig.~\ref{fig:curves} shows the two deprivation functions.
The \emph{everyday deprivation level} of a grid cell, between 0 and 1,
expresses how far the burden of reaching five everyday service categories
on foot has progressed toward saturation: near 0, all services are
comfortably walkable; near 1, the cell is effectively without walkable
everyday services and additional minutes no longer add burden. The
\emph{emergency deprivation cost} of a cell is an open-ended positive
number expressing the welfare lost through its drive time to the nearest
emergency facility, in multiples of the loss at the 15-minute
emergency-medical-service (EMS) benchmark: a cell at 2.0 carries twice the
deprivation of one whose ambulance arrives at the benchmark, and the cost
keeps escalating beyond it. ``Cost'' here is welfare lost, never money.
Both curves are anchored on external standards rather than fitted to the
data: the 15-minute-city threshold for the everyday level, and the EMS
response benchmarks for the emergency cost.

The level and the cost are different objects because the needs are, and
deprivation theory says why. The welfare lost during time without a good
depends on whether the good can be substituted or deferred
\citep{holguinveras2013objective}. Everyday services can: a further
pharmacy, a later trip or a delivery absorbs the loss, so the marginal
minute loses value once coping sets in and the loss is bounded. Emergency
care cannot: survival worsens with every minute
\citep{larsen1993survival,nicholl2007distance}, so the marginal minute
gains value and the loss is unbounded. A single loss function for both
would either declare a 60-minute ambulance no worse than a 30-minute one
or declare a distant supermarket a catastrophe. Relative to the calibrated
curves, the linear loss of a travel-time average over-prices minutes where
they are welfare-inert (the saturated everyday range) and under-prices
them where they are clinically decisive (around the emergency benchmarks).
The asymmetry imposes one discipline used throughout: only unit-invariant
statistics cross regimes (elasticities, Ginis, rank-based measures), and
raw magnitudes never do.

\subsection{Deprivation is a different object from access}\label{sec:depvsaccess}

The first result is that the deprivation layer changes what is seen. Every
headline regression of this paper is estimated twice, once on the
deprivation surfaces and once on the deprivation-free, minutes-based
indicators the accessibility literature would use
\citep{nicoletti2023disadvantaged,wu2025global}, and the comparison has
three parts (Fig.~\ref{fig:depvsaccess}; exact values in
Table~\ref{tab:si-depvsaccess}).

Deprivation is the better-behaved object. City size explains 44\,\% of the
cross-city variance in mean everyday deprivation against 16\,\% for the
median walk minute, its access counterpart (Fig.~\ref{fig:depvsaccess}A).
The reason is the saturating level function: whether a periphery cell sits
50 or 70 minutes from a pharmacy moves a travel-time average and moves no
household's daily routine, and the level function discounts that
variation. Anchored deprivation acts as a welfare-weighted filter on
travel time, and the filtered outcome is the one that scales cleanly. The
filtering costs nothing in ordering: the deprivation-based and time-based
Gini families rank cities almost identically (Pearson $r = 0.965$ everyday,
$0.976$ emergency), which is why every rank-based result in this paper can
be called parameterisation-free, and why the level and inequality results
are where the calibration carries information that travel time cannot.

Deprivation reveals what travel-time averages hide. Bucharest's median
emergency drive time is 8.5 minutes, 0.71 times the sample median, so by
the standard access statistic the city is better served than typical urban
Europe. Its mean emergency deprivation cost is 2.81 times the sample value
(Fig.~\ref{fig:depvsaccess}B; Table~\ref{tab:deserts}). Four other
capitals, Vilnius, Ljubljana, R\={\i}ga and Tallinn, show the same
anatomy: median-time ratios of 0.71--1.92 coexist with deprivation ratios
of 2.45--4.72, because a well-served core pulls the median minute down
while a deep periphery, where drive times run far beyond the 15-minute
benchmark, carries convex costs that no average over uniform minutes can
register. These five cities are the emergency deserts of
Section~\ref{sec:coverage}, and only a loss function that prices the tail
the way the clinical benchmarks say it should reveals them. Even inside the
access paradigm the signal sits in the tail: the only minutes-based
emergency statistic to reach conventional significance in the size
regressions is the 90th-percentile drive time ($-0.059$, $p = 0.017$), the
most tail-sensitive access statistic in the set.

Deprivation agrees with access wherever the result is about ranks, and
that agreement protects the findings below from the suspicion of being
calibration artefacts. The everyday size gradient is present in plain
minutes (median walk-time elasticity $-0.237$, $p = 3.8\times10^{-5}$;
mean walk time $-0.178$), and the absence of an emergency gradient is
present in minutes too (elasticities $-0.043$ to $-0.059$ across median,
mean and 90th-percentile drive time). No headline of
Sections~\ref{sec:scaling}--\ref{sec:coverage} depends on the
deprivation functions; the functions restate in welfare terms a structure
the network geometry already carries, and add the levels, the inequality
magnitudes and the tails.

\subsection{Larger cities have lower everyday deprivation, but not lower
emergency deprivation}\label{sec:scaling}

If the returns to scale behind urban service provision were general, as
scaling theory holds for infrastructure
\citep{bettencourt2007growth,bettencourt2013origins}, both deprivations
should fall with city size. The everyday regime behaves as that account
predicts (Fig.~\ref{fig:scaling}A). Mean everyday deprivation falls
systematically with population: the log-log elasticity is $-0.196$
(country-clustered SE 0.023, \pw{0.0002}), so mean everyday deprivation
falls by roughly 19\,\% for each e-fold ($\approx 2.7\times$) of
population, and city size alone explains 44\,\% of the cross-city
variance. The mechanism is visible beneath the deprivation surfaces: the
median FUA routes its population to 93 mapped general practitioners, 228
pharmacies, 325 supermarkets, 409 schools and 636 green spaces, the
population-weighted median walk to the nearest facility of each everyday
category is 4--8 minutes, and larger cities carry denser facility
networks. The estimate is stable under country fixed effects ($-0.17$),
under leave-one-out city deletion (envelope $-0.204$ to $-0.185$, with
Athina the most influential city), under outlier-robust estimators (Huber
$-0.196$, median regression $-0.223$) and under exclusion of the five
emergency-desert capitals ($-0.203$), so no single city, country or
estimator choice carries it.

The emergency regime breaks the pattern. Mean emergency deprivation cost
shows no detectable size gradient: the elasticity is $-0.059$ (clustered
$p = 0.16$, \pw{0.21}), and city size explains 2\,\% of the cross-city
variance. Non-significance alone would be weak evidence, so the null is
bounded: two one-sided equivalence tests place the emergency elasticity
within $\pm 0.13$ \citep{lakens2017equivalence}. The bound licenses a
precise statement and no more: any emergency size gradient, if present, is
at most two-thirds the everyday one in magnitude; equivalence at the
stricter $\pm 0.10$ bound is not shown, so the claim is ``no detectable
scaling, bounded within $\pm 0.13$'', never ``proven flat''. The
descriptive layer shows why: the median FUA has 6 emergency departments
and 6 ambulance stations for its whole territory, the median
population-weighted drive to either is 12 minutes, and the facility count
of a national emergency system does not multiply with city population the
way pharmacies do. The two slopes also differ from each other, tested
within cities rather than by comparing two separate regressions: a paired
regime test (regime $\times$ log-population interaction, country-clustered)
puts the everyday slope $0.137$ below the emergency slope (\pw{0.002}).
Agglomeration benefits are therefore regime-specific, and no single
exponent describes what city size does to service deprivation.

Within-city inequality moves in the opposite direction
(Fig.~\ref{fig:scaling}B). The Gini of everyday deprivation rises with
population: elasticity $+0.062$ (\pw{0.0012}). The emergency Gini is
size-flat at the baseline parameterisation ($+0.004$, \pw{0.84}), and the
paired slope difference of $-0.058$ is significant over all 67 cities
(\pw{0.029}). Size therefore buys lower mean everyday deprivation at the
price of a wider spread around that mean, while emergency inequality is
set elsewhere. The everyday half of the claim is defended by a
specification curve \citep{simonsohn2020specification} rather than by any
single calibration (Fig.~S\ref{fig:si-speccurve}): re-estimating the slope
under all 14 deprivation parameterisations keeps it positive throughout
($+0.026$ to $+0.075$) and significant in 13 of 14; the exception is the
concave Box-Cox everyday form swap ($+0.026$, $p = 0.083$). The emergency
half is conditional, and the conditionality is a finding. The EMS
benchmarks define the cost scale up to the 15-minute cut-off, where all
three literature-grounded escalation forms coincide; beyond the cut-off no
benchmark exists, and there the forms diverge (Fig.~\ref{fig:curves}C).
Under the bounded survival-based curve the emergency Gini is size-flat
($+0.001$, $p = 0.97$); under polynomial Box-Cox escalation, the published
baseline, it is size-flat across the whole curvature sweep ($-0.006$ to
$+0.014$, none significant), in agreement with the deprivation-free
travel-time Gini, itself size-flat ($-0.015$, $p = 0.42$); under
exponential escalation it rises with size ($+0.098$, $p = 0.0003$),
because exponential pricing turns the Gini into an index of extreme-tail
depth (it saturates at a median of 0.96, decouples from the time
distribution's inequality, $\rho = 0.04$, and tracks the beyond-45-minute
population share, $\rho = 0.45$), and how many minutes past the cut-off the
worst-served residents sit does grow with size even though the tail's
population share does not (beyond-30-minute share elasticity $-0.003$,
$p = 0.99$). Two statements hold under every parameterisation: everyday
inequality rises with size, and emergency inequality never falls with
size. Whether emergency inequality is flat or rising is decided by the
beyond-benchmark escalation form, which no clinical benchmark pins down;
it is flat under two of the three hypotheses and rising under the third.

\subsection{Emergency deprivation follows national emergency-system
coverage, not city size}\label{sec:coverage}

City size explains 2\,\% of the variance in mean emergency deprivation
cost. Most of the remaining variance lies between countries: in a nested
decomposition of the 67 city values, 25\,\% of the variance sits between
the four macro-regions, a further 54\,\% between countries within regions,
and only 22\,\% between cities of the same country
(Table~\ref{tab:si-variance}). The same holds for the inequality
indicators: the country level accounts for 70\,\% of the variance in the
emergency Gini, 75\,\% in the everyday Gini and 76\,\% in the divergence
gap, against 40\,\% for the coupling and compounding measures. We first
identify the structure in the city vectors themselves, then ask what
geography it has, and then test it at the level at which it varies, the
country.

\paragraph{A coverage ordering, not a typology.}
The 67 cities do not fall into types. k-means clustering on the full
standardised city vector separates 62 cities from 5 (silhouette 0.65,
bootstrap ARI 0.87 \citep{hubert1985comparing,rousseeuw1987silhouettes},
Gaussian-null $p = 0.001$), and the isolated group is an outlier group
rather than a type: its agreement with the region partition is
ARI $= 0.001$. Those five are the emergency-desert capitals of
Section~\ref{sec:depvsaccess}, separated by their beyond-threshold
emergency travel-time tails. Ward clustering selects the same axis and cuts
it more conservatively, isolating a nested subset of the two most extreme
capitals (65/2). The nesting is evidence for a gradient: the two algorithms
disagree about where to cut a severity ordering and agree on the ordering
itself. Re-clustering with the outliers removed finds one further split
along the same axis at lower intensity, a 48/14 cut isolating partial-desert
cities with real emergency tails (among them Oslo, Stockholm, Warszawa,
Sofia, Zagreb, Palermo and Luxembourg; silhouette 0.38, bootstrap ARI 0.74,
null $p = 0.001$, ARI vs region 0.07), inside which Ward again nests
(56/6). A feature-set robustness check that re-runs the whole pipeline
with the 8- and 15-minute benchmark shares added reproduces the main
partition exactly (ARI 1.00) and the peeled cut at ARI 0.97, while flagging
Br\u{a}ila and \L{}om\.za, the two smallest CEE cities, as sitting on the
covered/partial boundary. The citable structure is therefore a
one-dimensional \emph{coverage ordering} of the emergency periphery,
covered (48) $\rightarrow$ partial desert (14) $\rightarrow$ desert (5),
with a soft boundary between covered and partial and a robust ordering. We
call these coverage grades and never city types. A city's grade expresses
how much of its population lives beyond the emergency response benchmark:
covered cities have a thin tail, partial deserts a real one, deserts a
periphery in which drive times run far past the 15-minute target.

The coverage grade, and no other variable we test, sets the emergency
level. In a single country-clustered model with ln population centred at
the sample mean, the grade dummies shift mean emergency deprivation cost
overwhelmingly (Wald $p \approx 1.3\times10^{-15}$); the median mean cost
rises 1.02 $\rightarrow$ 1.87 $\rightarrow$ 3.37 multiples of \gref{}
across covered $\rightarrow$ partial $\rightarrow$ desert
(Fig.~\ref{fig:grade}). The slope-by-grade interaction is $p = 0.010$
under the current 48/14/5 grades but was $p = 0.17$ under the pre-refresh
50/12/5 grades; a two-city boundary change flips it, so we treat the
interaction as boundary-sensitive and do not headline any grade-specific
size gradient. Consistent with this, the divergence-gap measures show at
most a weak size gradient (gap slope $-0.066$ per $\log_{10}$ population,
$p = 0.012$, $R^2 = 0.06$; Fig.~S\ref{fig:si-sizegradient}), so size is a
poor predictor of where the two regimes part ways.

\paragraph{The geography of the ordering.}
The coverage ordering has a geography (Fig.~\ref{fig:map}). All five
deserts are capitals of Central-Eastern European (CEE) countries. The
partial-desert grade is a Nordic and CEE phenomenon: 7 of the 13 Nordic
cities and 5 of the 20 CEE cities carry a real emergency tail, against 1 of
16 in the West (Luxembourg) and 1 of 18 in the South (Palermo)
(Table~\ref{tab:si-grade-region}). West and South are almost entirely
covered. The two partitions, grade and region, are therefore different
(ARI 0.001 and 0.07) and related: the emergency tail that defines the
grades is a feature of the sparsely settled peripheries of Nordic FUAs and
of the national emergency systems of Central-Eastern Europe. In the
inequality plane (Fig.~\ref{fig:cityplane}) the five deserts are the five
highest points on the emergency-Gini axis and the partial deserts fill the
band below them, so the emergency-side inequality of a city is, to a first
approximation, its coverage grade.

\paragraph{Regional contrasts, tested at the country level.}
Because macro-region is a property of countries, whole countries are
permuted across regions in every regional test (Fig.~\ref{fig:regions}).
The emergency Gini differs by macro-region (Cohen $f = 0.74$,
\pperm{0.0024}), as do the divergence gap (\pperm{0.010}), the
everyday--emergency coupling $\rho$ (\pperm{0.0015}) and both compounding
measures (\pperm{0.0008} for the HH share, \pperm{0.0023} for the
continuous intensity). Central-Eastern Europe compounds: the region pairs
the highest emergency inequality (median emergency Gini 0.54) with the
strongest coupling between the two surfaces (median $\rho = 0.55$), so the
same populations tend to carry both deprivations. The West's inequality
problem is everyday-side (median divergence gap $-0.12$); the North's is
emergency-side ($+0.09$). These contrasts are not an artefact of the five
deserts: re-testing without them leaves every CEE contrast in place
(coupling \pperm{0.003}, emergency Gini \pperm{0.006}, divergence gap
\pperm{0.035}, HH share \pperm{0.0016}, compounding intensity
\pperm{0.0023}; Table~\ref{tab:si-nodeserts}). The regions also differ in
how dispersed their cities are: the spread of the emergency Gini and of
the divergence gap is about twice as wide in Central-Eastern Europe as
elsewhere (standard deviation 0.13 against 0.06--0.08 for the emergency
Gini), and the difference in dispersion survives country permutation
(\pperm{0.028} and \pperm{0.010}; Table~\ref{tab:si-dispersion}), while
the dispersion of the coupling and of the everyday Gini does not differ
regionally.

The everyday Gini's apparent regional contrast does not survive
country-level testing (\pperm{0.27} despite a city-level ANOVA
$p = 0.0007$), and Fig.~\ref{fig:regions} shows why. The Southern cities
split into two groups with no overlap: the seven Spanish cities and Athina
have everyday Ginis of 0.50--0.61, the ten Italian and Portuguese cities
0.27--0.42 (Table~\ref{tab:si-south}). Half of the variance in the
everyday Gini lies between countries within regions, more than for any
other indicator, so the ``regional'' contrast is a country contrast. It
coincides with a known mapping artefact: primary care in Portugal and
Italy sits in health centres that OpenStreetMap tags differently from
individual practices, so mapped general-practitioner density there is a
fifth of the Western median (Section~\ref{sec:limitations}), which
overstates everyday deprivation levels and compresses their within-city
spread. The paper therefore makes no regional everyday-Gini claim and no
cross-country comparison of everyday levels; the everyday size gradients
are unaffected, because mapped GP density is uncorrelated with city size.

\subsection{Compounding is the norm, and children carry
it}\label{sec:compounding}

The co-location typology measures how often the same residents lose in
both regimes. It cuts both percentile surfaces at their population-weighted
medians, which fixes the marginals at one half each; the whole 2$\times$2
class table then has a single free number, the high-high (HH) compounding
share, and statistical independence of the two surfaces corresponds to
HH $= 25\,\%$. Everything above 25\,\% is excess co-location. The observed
HH share averages 33\,\% and exceeds independence in 66 of 67 cities; the
rank coupling $\rho$ between the two surfaces spans $-0.02$ to $0.74$
(Fig.~S\ref{fig:si-rho}). Compounding is not an exotic overlap of two rare
conditions; across urban Europe it is the default relationship between
the two deprivations.

Thresholded shares invite the question of the threshold, so the
robustness harness prices it: the ``how high is high'' percentile cut
moves the HH share by 29.8 percentage points at the median city, against
1.4 under the whole deprivation-curvature grid and 0.9 under the
functional-form swaps, a roughly 21-fold dominance of the analyst's
threshold over the welfare calibration. Two consequences follow. First,
the headline compounding measure is the threshold-free continuous
intensity, the population-weighted mean depth to which the typical
resident sits inside \emph{both} percentile distributions, with fixed
anchors (1/3 under independence, 1/2 under perfect coupling, 1/4 under
perfect divergence); it shows the same regional structure as the
thresholded share (\pperm{0.0023}, strongest in Central-Eastern Europe).
Second, every thresholded share in this paper travels with its threshold
sweep. The spatial anatomy is consistent across contrasting cities
(Fig.~\ref{fig:gallery}): compounding concentrates in peripheries where
the saturated everyday level meets the escalating emergency cost, while
dense cores hold most population in the low-low class. The maps are read
under two rules stated once: legends carry population-weighted shares
while the picture is area-weighted (a sparse periphery dominates visually
at a modest population share), and no single cell's class is a finding
(roughly 24\,\% of population flips class between routing engines); the
per-city maps for all 67 cities are in the SI gallery.

The vulnerability analysis asks who lives in the compounded areas.
Following the social-vulnerability tradition \citep{cutter2003vulnerability}, vulnerability enters on two
strictly separated levels: EU-harmonised census age strata, comparable
across all 67 cities and pooled; and national fine-grid strata (rent,
tenure, finer ages), which are per-city depth and never pooled. Each
stratum is compared against the cells where its own census variable is
published (the covered-cell reference), because data coverage has its own
geography. At the harmonised level the result is one-sided: cells in the
top quartile of child share carry more everyday deprivation than their
comparison cells in 88\,\% of cities (median ratio 1.27) and more
compounding in 88\,\% (median HH-share gap $+0.13$), strongest in
Central-Eastern Europe (median ratio 1.44) and the North (1.30)
(Fig.~\ref{fig:vuln}). The elderly show no such pattern at the European
level: median ratio 0.96, above parity in only 37\,\% of cities, below
parity across most of Central-Eastern Europe. That null is itself a
result, and it is scale-specific: the national-data tier resolves sharper
per-city patterns (in Hamburg, elderly 1.37, children 1.52, low-rent 1.61
against covered references), but those use different definitions and
stay per-city. The European claim is about children. Whatever sorting
mechanism places families with children in the compounded periphery
operates consistently across nearly the whole sample, in a group whose
daily radius is short and whose emergency risk is real.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}
% ==========================================================================

\subsection{Deprivation as the welfare measure of urban proximity}\label{sec:theory-deprivation}

The paper's first contribution is to bring deprivation theory from
humanitarian logistics \citep{holguinveras2013objective,holguinveras2016econometric}
into the analysis of a functioning city, and the results show what the
transfer buys. Where results are rank-based, travel time and deprivation
agree almost perfectly ($r \geq 0.965$ between the Gini families), which
certifies the accessibility literature's rank-level findings
\citep{nicoletti2023disadvantaged,wu2025global} at continental scale.
Where results concern levels, inequality magnitudes or tails, travel time
was never a welfare measure, and the divergence is not noise: the desert
capitals sit at 0.71--1.92 times the sample by access and at 2.45--4.72
times it by deprivation (Table~\ref{tab:deserts}), and the choice of
measure decides whether an emergency-side size story exists at all. The
theory also explains why the two regimes are different objects. A bounded
level for substitutable needs and an unbounded cost for time-critical
needs are what deprivation theory implies for the two kinds of good, and
the clinical response literature \citep{larsen1993survival,nicholl2007distance}
supplies the anchors that transport economics' value of time, estimated on
reschedulable trips, cannot. The framework (forms transferred from an
estimated literature, curvature calibrated to domain anchors, robustness
of ranks separated from the meaning of levels) travels to any domain that
possesses anchors of comparable strength.

\subsection{What urban growth delivers, regime by regime}\label{sec:theory-scaling}

Scaling theory's power rests on exponents that hold across cities, systems
and quantities, with infrastructure in a single sublinear universality
class \citep{bettencourt2007growth,bettencourt2013origins}; its recent
refinements condition exponents on the stage of a national urban system
\citep{musso2026cities}. Our results add a different kind of condition:
the exponent depends on the regime of the service class, within one urban
system and one harmonised measurement. Everyday deprivation scales
($-0.196$, explaining 44\,\% of cross-city variance); emergency deprivation
does not (bounded within $\pm 0.13$); and the two slopes differ within
cities (\pw{0.002}). A single infrastructure exponent is therefore not a
sufficient statistic for what city size does to service deprivation, and
readings of agglomeration as generalised provisioning gain overstate what
growth delivers. In a mature urban system, where large cities no longer
grow faster than small ones \citep{musso2026cities}, what size still
delivers is lower everyday deprivation. The inequality result cuts
deeper: everyday inequality rises with size under every parameterisation
tested, so the same agglomeration that lowers mean everyday deprivation
widens its spread. Scaling theory has largely concerned means; these
results argue that the scaling of within-city distributions, and
specifically of welfare-weighted distributions, is where the next
generation of urban scaling laws has to live. The 15-minute-city paradigm
\citep{moreno2021fifteenminute,allam2022fifteenminute} emerges recast: its
threshold anchors the everyday regime well, and precisely because that
regime saturates, a proximity paradigm is an everyday-services paradigm,
silent about the escalating-cost regime and about the compounded
populations where both fail.

\subsection{Built for the everyday, tested by the emergency}\label{sec:theory-resilience}

Resilience research has moved toward defining resilience as equitable
access to essential services \citep{logan2020reframing,meerow2016defining},
has documented that access equality and network resilience reinforce each
other \citep{fan2022equality}, and has shown everyday vulnerability
failing to predict crisis capability \citep{sirenko2026heatwaves}. Our
two-regime measurement gives that distinction quantitative form across an
urban system. The everyday and emergency surfaces are distinct objects
with different scaling laws and different geographies: the first is set
by the city and its density, the second by the coverage of a national
emergency system, so a city's resilience cannot be read from either
surface alone, and a city built for the everyday regime is not thereby
resilient. The compounding result identifies the case the coupled-systems
argument predicts: the same neighbourhoods in the periphery of both
systems, above statistical independence in 66 of 67 cities, strongest
where emergency inequality is highest (Central-Eastern Europe), and
consistently the neighbourhoods with many children. Compounding
intensity, with its fixed anchors under independence and perfect coupling,
is offered as a general-purpose measure of that coupling for any pair of
capability surfaces. The measurement is of the emergency regime under
normal conditions, the floor from which any disruption starts. That floor
matters more each year: exposure to extreme heat in Europe is projected to
rise from ten million to almost 300 million people a year
\citep{naumann2020heat}, heat mortality to triple
\citep{garcialeon2024mortality}, and the Preparedness Union Strategy
\citep{ec2025preparedness} now asks member states to plan hospitals,
schools and transport for the emergency regime explicitly. A geography of
emergency deprivation measured before the disruption is the baseline such
planning needs.

\subsection{Implications for planning and preparedness}\label{sec:policy}

The practical reading is short, because the two regimes point at two
different jurisdictions. Everyday inequality is a big-city problem: it
rises with size under every parameterisation, so the cities gaining
everyday proximity on average are the ones whose internal spread widens,
and intra-urban service planning is the lever. Emergency deprivation is a
national coverage problem: it follows coverage grade rather than size,
differs by country and macro-region, and its extreme cases are five
capital cities whose peripheries no municipal instrument can serve alone.
The coverage ordering gives national emergency-system planners a
screening variable that access averages do not provide, and the
Preparedness Union Strategy gives it an address. The consistent carriers
of compounded deprivation at the European level are families with
children, in 88\,\% of the cities studied, which makes school- and
family-anchored service planning a compounding remedy rather than a
sectoral one.

\subsection{Limitations}\label{sec:limitations}

Every size gradient is cross-sectional; the space-for-time reading was
flagged once and applies throughout. The analysis covers walking for
everyday services and driving for emergency care; public transit is
deliberately out of scope, and the everyday regime is walk-only by
design. Free-flow civilian drive times anchor the emergency cost scale and
are not literal response-time predictions; disruption itself is not
modelled, so the emergency surface is the pre-disruption baseline. Census
strata arrive on a 1\,km grid broadcast to the 100\,m analysis grid, so
they are neighbourhood attributes; employment is unreported in the census
grid for Germany and France, so no harmonised SES slope exists there by
design. Per-cell typology classes carry a roughly 24\,\% routing-engine
flip share and are read only as spatial patterns. Population shares beyond
fixed time thresholds contain the finite-fill constant assigned to
service-deprived cells and are footnoted wherever used. The emergency-side
external benchmark (INKAR) is unavailable, and the German registry counts
carry a verify-before-publication flag.

The deprivation layer itself is the first thing a reader should attack,
and SI~\ref{si:robustness} reports the attack in full. In short: levels
move with the calibration and are meant to (curvature alone moves a
typical city's everyday Gini by 0.24 Gini points, which is why every slope
claim is defended by the specification curve rather than one
parameterisation), while ranks and classes do not (the same curvature
moves the compounding share by 1.4 percentage points, against 29.8 for the
threshold choice), and the two emergency escalation swaps are the scoped
exceptions that change what the emergency Gini measures.

Two OpenStreetMap completeness specifics deserve statement rather than
smoothing. Mapped GP density varies about 12-fold across countries in a
way that reflects tagging conventions rather than health systems: the
median FUA has 10.8 mapped GPs per 100k inhabitants, yet the country
medians are 2.1 in Sweden, 2.3 in Portugal, 2.6 in Finland and 3.5 in
Italy against 24.5 across the West, because primary care there sits in
health centres OSM tags differently. Everyday deprivation levels are
correspondingly overstated in those countries, so cross-country everyday
\emph{level} comparisons are not supported, and the Southern split of
Section~\ref{sec:coverage} (Spain and Greece above Italy and Portugal) is
the visible consequence. The size gradients are unaffected: mapped GP
density is uncorrelated with city size (log-log slope $+0.09$,
country-clustered $p = 0.29$), and every scaling claim is
country-clustered. Separately, eight cities have only one of the two
emergency services mapped (Athina, Br\u{a}ila, Helsinki, Lahti,
Norrk\"oping, Szeged, Talavera de la Reina, \v{Z}ilina); the composite
renormalises weights over the services present, their median mean
emergency cost is 0.197 against 0.169 for the rest, the expected direction
at a modest size, and none of them is in the desert group, so no headline
turns on it. Table~\ref{tab:si-cities} reports the count per city.

\section{Materials and Methods}\label{sec:methods}
% ==========================================================================

\paragraph{Design and sample.}
Cross-sectional multi-city design with one harmonised city definition
(Eurostat/OECD functional urban areas) and trajectories read from the
cross-sectional size gradient
\citep{musso2026cities,bettencourt2007growth}; no longitudinal component
exists, and all outputs are labelled space-for-time. The sample is a
stratified draw over 4 macro-regions $\times$ 4 population strata: 67
FUAs, 24 countries, 113.9 million residents, 101{,}050 to 12.9 million
inhabitants (Table~\ref{tab:sample}; Table~\ref{tab:si-cities}).

\paragraph{Grid, facilities, routing.}
The analysis grid is the GHS-POP 100\,m population grid
\citep{schiavina2023ghspop}, cells clipped to each FUA. Facilities come
from OpenStreetMap via Overpass queries with expiry-refreshed
extractions; Table~\ref{tab:services} summarises the routed facility
sets. Travel times are computed with the R5 routing engine via r5py
\citep{fink2022r5py} over the OSM street network: walking for the
everyday regime, driving for the emergency regime, with emergency
matrices reverse-routed (station-to-cell, the substantively correct
direction for ambulance response; measured car asymmetry median 1
minute). A friction-surface engine \citep{weiss2020healthcare} is
retained as a declared sensitivity variant after a two-city cross-check
showed city-specific, uncorrectable level bias (E.1).

\paragraph{Everyday deprivation (a level).}
Per service, travel time is congestion-adjusted by a two-step floating
catchment area (2SFCA) factor \citep{luo2003measures,luo2009enhanced}:
facilities serving many people relative to capacity inflate the
effective time of their users. A soft-minimum over reachable facilities
then rewards substitutability (several similar options count as slightly
closer than the nearest alone). The effective time passes through a
logistic deprivation level function \citep{wang2017deprivation} with
ceiling 1, inflection $t_0 = 15$ minutes (the 15-minute-city threshold
\citep{moreno2021fifteenminute}) and steepness $k = 0.2$ per minute; the
city surface is the weighted mean over the five service categories
(school and green-space sub-types weigh 0.5 each). Cells with no network
path to any facility are masked; routable cells beyond a service's
cutoff receive a large finite time so they stay on the map at
near-maximal deprivation.

\paragraph{Emergency deprivation (a cost).}
The emergency surface applies a convex Box-Cox deprivation cost function
\citep{cantillo2018discrete,delgadolindeman2019ambulance} with
$\lambda = 1.8$ (transferred; solving $\lambda$ from the anchors gives
1.89--1.95, inside the sensitivity sweep) to the drive time to the
nearest emergency facility. The anchors are the EMS response benchmarks:
cost negligible below the 3--4 minute ideal
\citep{pons2005response,vo2020vulnerable}, about a third of the
benchmark cost at the 8-minute urban standard (contested: no survival
effect for most patients in \citep{pons2005response}, supported in
\citep{blanchard2012response}; attributed via \citep{khan2026melbourne},
whose WHO 2020 reference is an unpinnable URL), and 1 at the 15-minute
upper target \citep{peleg2004ems,khan2026melbourne}; convexity is
supported independently by distance-mortality evidence
\citep{nicholl2007distance}. The 8-minute mark cannot carry a half-max
anchor: convexity caps $g(8)/g(15)$ at 0.427 given $g(4)/g(15) = 0.10$,
so the two regimes' anchor structures are genuinely different. All costs
are reported in multiples of $\gref$. Free-flow civilian drive times
anchor the cost scale and are not literal response-time predictions
(dispatch and turnout are unmodelled; lights-and-siren travel is faster
than civilian flow; free-flow understates congested cores, which is
conservative for the periphery findings).

\paragraph{Cross-regime standardisation.}
The everyday level and the emergency cost are on incomparable scales by
design. Only unit-invariant statistics cross regimes: elasticities,
Ginis (mean-normalised, the same move as comparing an income Gini with a
wealth Gini) and rank-based measures. The co-location typology is built
on population-weighted mid-rank percentile surfaces; at the median split
the 2$\times$2 class table has one free number, the HH share, and
statistical independence corresponds to HH $= 25\,\%$. The continuous
compounding intensity is the population-weighted mean of the smaller of
the two percentile ranks (1/3 independence, 1/2 perfect coupling, 1/4
perfect divergence). The divergence gap (emergency Gini minus everyday
Gini) is the one cross-regime subtraction, a difference of dimensionless
indices, form-dependent, and never a standalone headline.

\paragraph{Equity strata.}
Census-2021 1\,km strata (broad age groups) are broadcast to the grid
and pooled across cities against covered-cell references (cells where
the stratum's variable is published); national fine-grid strata (rent,
tenure) are per-city depth and never pooled. Inequality within strata
uses the concentration index \citep{wagstaff1991measurement}.

\paragraph{Cross-city inference.}
Cities nest in 24 countries, so scaling regressions report
country-clustered standard errors with wild cluster bootstrap p-values
(null imposed) \citep{cameron2008bootstrap,mackinnon2023cluster};
regional contrasts are tested by permuting whole countries across
macro-regions, with a country-random-intercept mixed model as
triangulation; city-level ANOVA p-values are anti-conservative for such
questions and are never cited as evidence. The emergency null is bounded
by TOST equivalence tests \citep{lakens2017equivalence}; slope
robustness uses a specification curve over all 14 deprivation
parameterisations \citep{simonsohn2020specification}; clustering
diagnostics report silhouette \citep{rousseeuw1987silhouettes},
bootstrap and cross-partition adjusted Rand indices
\citep{hubert1985comparing} and a Gaussian silhouette null. Every
headline elasticity carries leave-one-out, Cook's-distance, Huber and
median-regression diagnostics.

\paragraph{Regional structure.}
Three additive analyses characterise where the cross-city variance lies
(\texttt{paper/analysis\_regional\_structure.py}; SI~\ref{si:regional}).
A nested sum-of-squares decomposition splits each indicator's variance
across the 67 cities into between-region, between-country-within-region
and within-country shares. Dispersion by region is compared with a
Fligner--Killeen test, whose statistic is re-evaluated under the same
country permutation used for the medians. Every regional contrast is
re-run without the five desert capitals with the same permutation test.

\paragraph{Robustness harness.}
A structured (non-probabilistic) sweep recomputes rank-based targets
over a defensible envelope: Layer 1 varies curvature within fixed forms;
Layer 2 swaps the forms while pinning the same anchors (concave Box-Cox
everyday; exponential and bounded survival-based emergency escalations);
Layer 3 sweeps the accessibility model (soft-min $\kappa$, congestion
exponent $\gamma$, bandwidth, $k$-nearest, mode set, unreachable
policy); a threshold axis sweeps the typology cut. Results are reported
as rank agreement, class-share envelopes, flip-cell shares and the
specification curve (SI~\ref{si:robustness}; Fig.~S\ref{fig:si-architecture}).

\paragraph{Reproducibility.}
The pipeline is config-driven with provenance sidecars on every derived
matrix; all tables and figures in this paper trace to the persisted
67-city state (main-text figures are drawn from the pack tables and the
configuration by the \texttt{paper/make\_fig\_*.py} scripts) (every quoted number is checked against its source table
by an automated audit of 171 checks run in CI). Code, configuration and
the full result tables are available in the study repository.

\paragraph{Data availability.}
Population grid: GHS-POP R2023A \citep{schiavina2023ghspop}. Facilities
and street networks: OpenStreetMap. Census strata: Eurostat Census 2021
grid. Per-city and cross-city result tables, per-city maps and
sensitivity sweeps are persisted on the study repository's results
branch.

\section{Robustness of the deprivation layer}\label{si:robustness}

A paper whose contribution is a measurement layer must expect that layer
to be attacked first, and the answer has two halves that both need
stating. What is varied is drawn in Fig.~\ref{fig:curves}: the Layer-1
curvature grid ($k \in \{0.1, 0.2, 0.3\}$ per minute $\times$
$t_0 \in \{10, 15, 20\}$ minutes for the everyday logistic;
$\lambda \in \{1.4, 1.8, 2.2\}$ for the emergency Box-Cox) and the
Layer-2 form swaps calibrated to the same domain anchors (a concave
Box-Cox everyday level; exponential and bounded survival-based emergency
escalations), all against the linear loss of a pure-access average.

How far the results move is in Table~\ref{tab:envelopes}. Levels are not
robust, and are not meant to be: curvature alone moves a typical city's
everyday Gini by 0.24 Gini points (median envelope width across the 67
cities), which is why the calibration carries information and why
every slope claim is defended by the specification curve rather than by
one parameterisation. Ranks and classes are robust: the same curvature
grid moves the compounding share by 1.4 percentage points at the median,
the form swaps by 0.9, against 29.8 for the ``how high is high''
threshold. City-level Gini orderings survive with two scoped exceptions:
rank agreement with the baseline stays at $\rho \geq 0.90$ over the
curvature grid and the everyday form swap, while both emergency
escalation swaps reorder the emergency-Gini ranking (exponential
$\rho = 0.19$; bounded survival $\rho = 0.49$) precisely because each
changes what that Gini measures, a change of measurand reported as such
rather than noise.

The wider inventory reads as one block. Deprivation-variant flip cells,
the population whose typology class changes under any deprivation
parameterisation, average 18.1\,\% of city population (range
5.2--53.0\,\%), with the HH class share moving 2.1 percentage points on
average and 6.1 at most. The clustering survives its own feature-set
choice (main partition ARI 1.00, peeled 0.97 with benchmark shares
included). The routing-engine cross-check bounds the engine's leverage:
switching engines moves travel-time levels by 34--314\,\% and flips the
class of roughly 24\,\% of population per city, while aggregate class
shares move at most 0.7 percentage points; per-cell map readings are
therefore never findings, aggregate shares are. OSM completeness was
benchmarked against national registries where available (Germany:
pharmacies 0.976, hospitals 1.24) and the external INKAR benchmark
passed. Fig.~S\ref{fig:si-architecture} assembles the architecture in one view. The reading
discipline it supports ran through the whole paper: rank-based results
are near-invariant to the deprivation parameterisation, and level and
inequality results are where the calibration carries the information.

\section{Validation exercises}\label{si:validation}

\paragraph{E.1 Routing-engine cross-check.} Re-running full cities under
the friction-surface engine against the R5 baseline (Hamburg, K\"oln)
shows travel-time levels understated by 34--314\,\% with city-specific
Gini bias (emergency Gini $-30$\,\% in Hamburg, $-18.5$\,\% in K\"oln),
which demoted friction to sensitivity variant. Aggregate typology class
shares move $\leq 0.7$ pp and the divergence $\rho$ by $\leq 11\,\%$,
while roughly 24\,\% of population flips per-cell class; hence per-cell
map readings are never findings.

\paragraph{E.2 OSM completeness.} Country-level benchmark against
national registries where available: Germany, pharmacies ratio 0.976,
hospitals 1.24 (the hospital ratio carries a verify-before-publication
flag on the registry side). The per-city facility counts
(\path{accessibility_by_service_cities.csv}) provide the cross-country
completeness picture the single-country registry benchmark cannot, and
surface the GP tagging artefact discussed in the limitations.

\paragraph{E.3 External benchmark.} The German INKAR accessibility
indicators reproduce the study's German pharmacy and supermarket access
patterns (passed); no comparable emergency-side benchmark is available.

\paragraph{E.4 Resolution and E.5 face validation.} Quantile-curve
comparisons of regime times under both engines and per-anchor-city face
validation documents are maintained in the study repository.

\paragraph{Re-anchor verification.} The emergency reporting anchor moved
from $g(45)$ to $\gref$ in August 2026. For the three cities re-seeded
from stored artifacts the verification is exact: every rank-based
indicator is identical to machine precision (max $|\Delta| \approx
10^{-16}$) and the level ratio equals $g(45)/g(15) = 6.7307$ to
$10^{-15}$; the other 64 cities were byte-identical between the two
runs.
